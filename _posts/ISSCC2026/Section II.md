### 2.0 Integrating AI into applications, enriching life through technology innovation

Two complementary overview examples are discussed in this section, providing a glimpse not only at the application benefits but also at the distinct engineering challenges emerging as AI takes a central role in information and communication technology. Previously outlined high-level themes, such as tackling the rapid increase in AI computational load and its energy demand, as well as embedding specialized intelligence with the physical edge, are elaborated in the context of bringing technology to applications enriching everyone’s life.

#### 2.1 Agentic AI and physical AI augmenting humans in autonomous vehicles and beyond

AI isn’t just something that happens behind the scenes in massive cloud data centers, it is also something that is always with you, from the things you carry to the things that carry you. The vast variety of AI transformations means that the technological innovations required are equally diverse. Agentic AI and physical AI are complementary paths to augmenting human capability. For instance, from increasing advanced driver assistance to various levels of autonomous driving, the combination of software agents with physical AI presently supports the integration of human-machine interface (HMI) with autonomous control, and sensing and actuation (Fukuoka & Fang, 2025) (Schiefer, 2025) (Rus, 2025) (Hung & al., 2019). AI agents will drive autonomous reasoning, tool use, and interaction, while multi-modal physical AI supports perception, control, and actuation under safety and timing guarantees.

Over time, it is anticipated that the prevailing human-centric paradigm will shift toward a bot-centric one where bot includes both physical and non-physical AI, i.e. robots and agents, respectively. In turn, the technical differentiations between entities such as ‘robots’ and ‘vehicles’ will likely blur, and distinctions will become primarily functional, contingent upon specific use cases and operational domains rather than rigid, historical definitions. AI agents may serve as goal-directed copilots that can plan multi-step tasks, call tools (navigation, communications, commerce), query cloud/enterprise data, and converse with occupants showing natural and appropriate emotions, going beyond single-turn voice commands.

OEM pilots that embed large-model assistants into In-Vehicle Infotainment (IVI) stacks (e.g., Mercedes-Benz integrating ChatGPT via Azure OpenAI in MB’s UX) show the near-term trajectory toward richer in-car agents. Physical AI in vehicle is the embodied counterpart: from perception (from a rapidly growing number of cameras, LIDAR, to all types of sensors) to planning to control loops that actuate steering, braking, powertrain, and comfort functions under deterministic timing and functional safety governed by ISO 26262, further strengthened by cybersecurity (ISO/SAE 21434), OTA/software-update governance (UNECE R155/R156), safety of the intended function (SOTIF), and the overarching framework of IEC 61508.

Projection of navigation commands and traffic-aware Advanced Driver Assistance System (ADAS) cues into the driver’s view (e.g., Continental’s AR-HUD waveguide systems), crash avoidance, Drowsiness and Attention Warning (DDAW) and in-cabin monitoring of wellness will be a safety baseline. Managing conversation length between the agent and the driver, visual load, and AR cue density under driving state will require new regulations.

To support adequate data traffic within the vehicle’s units, achieving multi-hundred GB/p bandwidth with automotive-grade 3D packaging for performance, energy efficiency, cost and reliability while able to mitigate random failures for functional safety creates challenges before mainstream automotive use. To support a bot-centric paradigm, a centralized training and distributed-deployment methodology can simultaneously reduce communication data bandwidth, lower compute latency and improve scalability. The cloud handles compute-intensive pretraining and fine-tuning, while edge devices execute compact, fine-tuned models for low-latency, low-cost decision making (e.g., via federated learning in IoT scenarios (Albogami, 2025)). To balance data access across the cloud-edge synergy, leveraging hierarchical model deployment, bandwidth-aware model partitioning for inference, adaptive compression and quantization of features, and life-long learning of edge devices can substantially reduce the required data traffic (Kim, Pinyoanurlaphat, Kim, Saad, & Calin, 2025).

Physical AI delivering safe, precise assistance pushes compute to the edge under bounded-latency constraints. The IEEE Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN) suite provides time synchronization (802.1AS) and time-aware scheduling (802.1Qbv) to guarantee latency and jitter budgets on Ethernet, which is foundational for closed-loop perception-to-actuation chains. ISO 26262 for E/E functional safety (Debouk, 2019) complemented by ISO 21434 regulates the automotive domain and different categories of standards for cybersecurity, frameworks that shape controller architectures, redundancy, and runtime monitors. These requirements translate into local AI accelerators for perception, safety-rated controllers, and secure, deterministic networks, which are direct levers for physical productivity gains.

Meanwhile, cars have evolved far beyond simple transportation. They’re now fully connected mobile environments for work, business, and entertainment. Today’s vehicles are packed with high-performance multimedia systems that offer real-time online interaction and conferencing, immersive entertainment and gaming, seamless connectivity through both terrestrial (5G, 6G) and non-terrestrial networks (NTN) (Fu & al., 2023). This includes the car’s own system as well as the many independent mobile devices that passengers would bring with them into the car, all able to communicate and cooperate, as part of a local device cloud, directly among each other (Tenny, Kim, Shariat, Hsu, & Calin, 2025). Delivering this smooth, integrated user experience is a perfect blend of Agentic AI and Physical AI (Shih & al., 2024) (Hsieh & al., 2025). This powerful software and hardware combination runs on a high-performance, energy-efficient computing architecture, powered by cutting-edge chips built using advanced manufacturing processes, following a smart system-level design as those previously discussed (Varma & al., 2024) (MediaTek, 2025) (Tsai, 2025).

However current physical AI systems face significant security and privacy challenges, especially as they transition from digital agents to embodied platforms. Reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)-trained models may exhibit reward hacking, goal misalignment, and power-seeking behavior (Ngo, 2024). Strengthening governance of training data is essential to cover privacy, consent, and transparency (King & al., 2024). Several frameworks already guide AI development: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (RMF) provides methods to identify, assess, and manage AI risks; the EU AI Act sets legal requirements for deploying AI in the EU, and ISO/IEC 42001 offers guidance for organizations to structure policies and controls to manage AI. To further bridge the security gap, existing frameworks must be extended beyond digital risk management to include cyber-physical safeguards.

#### 2.2 Data centers expansion, and the transition from IC design to Multiphysics Design/System-Technology Co-Optimization (DTCO and STCO)

As mentioned, multiple inter-related trends emerge in the context of data centers, requiring DTCO and STCO of distinct engineering disciplines to tackle the growing demands for energy and the physical limits of materials, thermal management, the complexity of network connectivity, security and resilience. In this area, MediaTek is enabling the rapid infrastructure compute growth (“overwhelming compute”) by solving difficult technical obstacles with multiple leading technologies and through key technology partnerships (NVLink, 2025), reducing system power, total cost of ownership, while increasing performance (Nalamalpu, 2025) (Hu, 2025).

The large electrical power consumed by high-density servers leads to difficult thermal management challenges, pushing traditional air-cooling technologies past their physical limits, and leading to much more effective liquid-based solutions. These include direct-to-chip (D2C) cooling, where a coolant, typically water, circulates through cold plates (heat exchangers) mounted on top of hot components such as CPUs/GPUs, or flows among them in a 3-D heterogeneous integration system via micro-fluid cooling channels, absorbing heat at the source and transporting it away (Wu & al., 2025). Additionally, with the more aggressive immersion cooling, entire servers or other components are submerged into a tank filled with a dielectric fluid. Finally, to enhance efficiency and eliminate wasteful overcooling, AIOps (AI for IT Operations) algorithms have been deployed. They use predictive analytics on IT workload patterns to forecast cooling needs, dynamically adjusting airflow, water temperature, and pump speeds in real-time to manage hotspots and meet the precise demands of the IT load (Liu, Alpri; Song, J., & Hua, 2022).

At a “processor” level, heterogeneous integration of chiplets and multi-die system-in-package (SiP) technologies have become mainstream, while systems-on-wafer (SoW) are emerging: 3-D heterogeneous electrical and optical integrated systems including three-dimensional ICs (3DIC), custom HBM, interposer/substrate aim to minimize resistive copper electrical communication between the compute and the memory parts. Die-to-die communication is implemented with extra-short reach interfaces with data rates per pin doubling almost every three to four years across different I/O standards, including UCIe. However, at a server level, cloud architecture is evolving. The traditional 3-tier architecture of a data center consisting of core, aggregation and edge layers, suited for traditional client-server applications is disaggregating to a highly distributed structure required by large AI workloads, such as training LLMs, where continuous, high-bandwidth communication is required between hundreds or more servers simultaneously. Therefore, as interconnect rates increase, and to meet different distance/reach/channel loss and other physical requirements, distinct data interface solutions are required. DSP-based serial links beyond 2000Gb/s have been demonstrated (Chen & al., 2025) (Mostafa & al., 2025), with unabated throughput demand soon moving to 448Gb/s while improving energy efficiency. Since the advancement in package, connectors, and PCB technologies face difficult physical obstacles to keep up with rapidly growing needs, then SERDES designs implement signal processing and circuit innovations to close the gap (Ali & al., 2021). Another approach is offered by silicon photonics and ASICs co-integration, enabling throughput beyond the limitations of electrical interconnects. Co-packaged optics (CPO) emerge due to several intrinsic physical advantages of data rate, bandwidth density, energy efficiency, communication latency and reduced cross-talk. General optical connectivity consists of electrical integrated circuit (EIC) dies, including optical driver, transimpedance amplifier (TIA), and potentially retimer and DSP, photonic integrated circuits (PIC) such as modulator, photodetector, optical multiplexers, polarization control and optional laser source; advanced packaging and integration, fiber attach and optical coupling (Mehia, 2024) (Yang & Hung, 2025).

The architectural system-level disaggregation tackling dynamically varying and fine-grained power requirements impact power management and distribution similarly to the above thermal management. At board level, a hierarchical power management structure, starting at a high voltage (e.g. 48 to 54V) distributes through the servers, where it is then DC/DC converted down to 12V by intermediate bus converters (IBC). In turn the IBCs distribute supplies to smart power stages (SPS) on the backside of the packages. While at substrate and die level, complex power delivery networks (PDNs) are developed to manage varying loads and voltage drops in 3DICs by through-silicon vias (TSVs) and backside power delivery, coupled with dedicated integrated voltage regulation (IVR) (and possibly gating when units are temporarily powered down) for the individual processing units and interface circuitry, hierarchically tied to top level supply sources (Veloso & al., 2023) (Loh K. -H., 2025) (Zhang et al., 2024) (Shahriari, 2025) as shown in Fig. 8.
